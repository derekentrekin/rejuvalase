Why plasma? The mechanism behind the platform.

June 2026 · 6 minute read · Phoenix Bioscience formulation team

Most joint supplements are a list of ingredients. Corvex™ is a platform with passengers. The platform is spray-dried porcine plasma — and the reasoning behind it is worth explaining in plain English.

What spray-dried plasma is

Spray-dried porcine plasma (SDPP) is the protein fraction of porcine blood, separated and spray-dried into a stable powder. The process preserves a broad mix of functional proteins — albumin, growth factors, and, notably, immunoglobulins — in a form that survives storage and feeding.

SDPP is not a novelty ingredient. It has decades of use in animal nutrition, where it has been studied extensively for its effects on gut health and resilience during periods of stress.

What the evidence suggests

Across the animal-nutrition literature, dietary plasma proteins have been associated with support for gut barrier integrity, normal immune balance in the gut, and maintained intake and condition during stress. More recent canine work has explored plasma's role in supporting healthy inflammatory balance and recovery from exertion.

Note the verbs: associated with, supports, explored. That's not us being coy — it's what the evidence grade honestly permits. Plasma is a well-studied functional ingredient, not a therapeutic, and we describe it accordingly.

Why it anchors Corvex™

A working dog's season stresses more than joints. Travel, heat, and sustained exertion put load on the gut and immune system too — and a dog that goes off feed on a trial weekend isn't recovering, whatever else is in its bowl. Plasma is the base layer of Corvex™ because it addresses that foundation: the systems that everything else, including joint support, depends on.

On top of that platform ride eight actives chosen for the mobility lanes — Levagen+®, GlycOmega™, NMX1000®, UC-II®, glucosamine HCl, chondroitin sulfate, MSM, and hyaluronic acid. Each has its own rationale, and each gets its own explainer in this library.

The honest caveats

Supplements support normal function; they don't treat disease. Evidence in dogs is thinner than in production animals, and individual response varies. If your dog is lame, painful, or slowing down suddenly, that's a veterinary visit, not a chew. We'd rather tell you that here than let a label imply otherwise.
